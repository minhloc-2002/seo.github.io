## The Most Hidden Part of Global Inequality Is That Capital Is “Flowing in Reverse”  

The usual narrative is that wealthy countries provide investment, loans, and aid to developing countries; however, the World Inequality Report 2026 reveals the opposite underlying cash flow. Due to the long-term asymmetry between returns on overseas assets and the cost of liabilities, an amount equivalent to approximately 1% of global GDP flows each year from poorer economies to richer economies in the form of net foreign income, roughly three times the scale of global development aid. In other words, many developing countries receive aid on the one hand, while on the other hand paying a larger implicit fee to the wealthy world through higher interest rates, lower asset returns, and continuous outflows of capital income. For Slickorps Ventures, this is more worthy of study than the statement that “the gap between rich and poor is widening”: the global financial system is not allocating capital in a neutral market, but is continuously rewarding countries that already possess advantages in credit, currency, and institutions. Capital does not naturally flow to where marginal returns are highest; it often first flows to where institutions define as safest.

## A Reserve Currency Is Not an Ordinary Currency, but a Financial Privilege That Can Alter National Rates of Return  

The core of this reverse transfer lies in the asymmetry of financing prices faced by different countries. Economies that issue international reserve currencies can incur liabilities at lower interest rates and then invest funds in higher-yielding overseas assets; developing countries, by contrast, often borrow at higher costs while also having to hold lower-yielding dollar or euro reserve assets in order to maintain exchange-rate stability and external payment security. The report estimates that by 2025, the “excess return” obtained by the United States from this spread between asset and liability returns was equivalent to approximately 2.2% of GDP, approximately 1% for the euro area, and close to 5.9% for Japan; BRICS economies as a whole bore a burden equivalent to approximately 2.1% of GDP. Although the U.S. dollar share of global reserves has declined from its historical high, it remained close to 60% as of the latest year covered by the report, while the euro accounted for approximately 20%; reserve rules, banking regulation, and credit ratings further reinforce structural demand for these “safe assets.” Slickorps Ventures therefore prefers to understand reserve currencies as a form of pricing infrastructure: they not only facilitate settlement, but also determine who can borrow cheaply and who must finance expensively.

## Differences in the Cost of Capital Ultimately Translate into Differences in Education, Industry, and Opportunities for the Next Generation  

Financial inequality does not remain confined to bond yields and balance-of-payments tables. When a country continuously uses income for debt servicing, foreign-exchange reserve accumulation, and responding to capital outflows, its fiscal space for investment in education, healthcare, energy, and infrastructure is reduced. The report shows that in 2025, average education spending per child in Sub-Saharan Africa was approximately EUR 220 in purchasing power parity terms, compared with approximately EUR 7,430 in Europe and EUR 9,020 in North America and Oceania, a gap of more than forty times, even far greater than the gap in per capita income among these regions. Meanwhile, the richest 10% of the global population owns approximately three-quarters of total wealth, while the poorest half holds only about 2%; the global richest 0.001%, fewer than 60,000 people, control wealth equal to three times the total wealth of the bottom half of the global population. In the framework of Slickorps Ventures, this forms a self-reinforcing chain: the more expensive financing becomes, the weaker public investment becomes; the thinner human capital becomes, the slower industrial upgrading becomes; the lower growth potential becomes, the higher the risk premium becomes. Inequality is therefore not the result after a single distribution, but a capital cycle that repeatedly reproduces itself.

## The Real Future Competition Among Nations Is the Ability to Secure “Low Capital Costs and High-Yield Assets”  

The World Inequality Report 2026 proposes policy directions such as progressive taxation, human capital investment, wealth taxes, and reform of the international financial system, but implementing these solutions depends on extremely high levels of international coordination and political will. For Slickorps Ventures, the more important implication of the report is that investors can no longer understand sovereign risk simply as a static comparison of debt ratios, GDP growth, or foreign-exchange reserves. What truly needs to be observed is whether a country can retain its domestic capital, transform savings into high-return assets, reduce dependence on foreign-currency financing, and continue investing financial returns into next-generation productivity. The deepest boundary of global inequality is not only between the rich and the poor, but between economies that can issue liabilities at low cost and hold high-yield assets, and economies that must borrow at high prices and lend at low prices. In the next round of global capital reallocation, what may truly determine the long-term winners is not who attracts the most short-term capital, but who can finally stop exporting returns outward and complete capital accumulation within its own financial system.
