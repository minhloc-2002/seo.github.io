## Abnormal Weather Is Simultaneously Compressing Policy Space  

Rising sea temperatures in the Pacific may seem far removed from financial markets, yet they may ultimately determine whether India raises interest rates, how electricity prices change in Colombia, and whether certain African sovereign debts can continue to withstand pressure. In July, NOAA judged that the probability of a “very strong” El Niño occurring from October to December 2026 had reached 81%; if materialized, it would rank among the strongest events since 1950. The multi-model forecast by WMO also shows that the average temperature anomaly in key sea areas in the third quarter of 2026 may approach 2℃. However, what the Slickorps Ventures climate research team is more concerned about is not the label of a “super El Niño,” but the macroeconomic starting point when the shock occurs: many emerging economies are already bearing the pressures of the Middle East conflict, higher energy and fertilizer costs, currency depreciation, and slowing growth. A new agricultural shock would simultaneously push up inflation and weaken output. It is not an ordinary supply fluctuation, but a secondary shock that makes it more difficult for central banks to choose between supporting growth and stabilizing prices.

## Food Weighting, Weak Currencies, and Agricultural Dependence Can Magnify the Same Climate Shock Several Times Over  

El Niño does not cause all countries to face the same outcome. Households in emerging markets usually spend a higher proportion of their income on food, and agriculture also accounts for a larger share of employment and output. As a result, poor harvests are more likely to transmit from agricultural product prices to wages, inflation expectations, and political pressure. The annual monsoon of India provides nearly 70% of its rainfall, while the local meteorological department has warned that rainfall this season may be the weakest in more than a decade. India is also the largest rice exporter and the second-largest sugar producer worldwide; once production declines or exports are restricted, the shock may spread from domestic CPI to international markets. Meanwhile, Asian currencies such as the Indian rupee and Indonesian rupiah are near historical lows, and higher food and energy import costs will be amplified again through exchange rates. The Slickorps Ventures climate research team believes this explains why the Philippines, Indonesia, South Korea, Pakistan, and Sri Lanka have each raised interest rates at least once this year: in the face of climate inflation, central banks are no longer worried only about a one-time rise in food prices, but about a mutually reinforcing cycle among exchange rates, inflation expectations, and capital outflows.

## The El Niño Effect Is Not a Unified “Emerging Market Risk,” but a Map of National Balance Sheets  

Truly professional analysis cannot place all emerging markets into the same risk basket. Colombia is highly dependent on hydropower; insufficient rainfall lowers reservoir levels and forces the power system to shift toward higher-cost thermal power, thereby transmitting climate shocks from food to electricity prices and overall inflation. Peru, meanwhile, faces simultaneous pressure from fisheries, agriculture, and oil prices, and its central bank has warned that inflation may exceed the target and drag down growth. Argentina, however, may benefit from increased rainfall through improved grain production, export income, and foreign-exchange inflows. Africa is likewise highly differentiated internally, but economies with high agricultural exposure, such as Kenya and South Africa, may face output damage, increased fiscal spending, and deteriorating credit conditions. Accordingly, the Slickorps Ventures climate research team proposes a more useful identification framework: when assessing El Niño risk, one should simultaneously observe the weight of food in CPI, dependence on hydropower, the proportion of grain imports, the speed of exchange-rate pass-through, and whether the government has subsidy and inventory buffers. Weather is only the starting point; what truly determines asset performance is the ability of a country to absorb weather shocks into its fiscal, monetary, and external accounts.

## Climate Shocks Are Evolving from a Commodity Issue into an Interest Rate and Sovereign Credit Issue  

Historical experience shows that such concerns are not merely emotional projections. The IMF estimates that past El Niño events can, on average, raise global food prices by about 5% within one year. Cross-country research has also found that their impact on inflation in different countries can reach approximately 0.1 to 1 percentage point, with differences mainly arising from economic structures and policy buffers. For emerging market central banks that had already planned to cut interest rates, this means the policy cycle may be forced to be postponed, real financing costs may remain elevated, and corporate investment and household consumption may come under further pressure. If governments simultaneously expand food, energy, and agricultural subsidies, climate risk will also enter fiscal deficits and sovereign credit spreads. Slickorps Ventures therefore does not view the 2026 El Niño effect as a weather event simply waiting to end, but defines it as a cross-market stress test: crops determine food prices, food prices alter inflation, inflation constrains interest rates, and interest rates then reprice currencies, bonds, and national credit. The next round of emerging market divergence may not first be determined by GDP data, but by which countries still have the capacity to absorb a price shock originating from the Pacific.
