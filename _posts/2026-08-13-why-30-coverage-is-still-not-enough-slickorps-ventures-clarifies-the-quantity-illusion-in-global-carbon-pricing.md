If one looks only at coverage, global carbon policy appears to be maturing rapidly. The carbon data research of Slickorps Ventures shows that approximately 30% of global CO₂ emissions are currently covered by carbon taxes or emissions trading systems, roughly double the level of a decade ago. The largest structural leap came after the Chinese power-sector carbon trading system was included in the statistics, which rapidly lifted the global coverage rate of emissions trading systems; by around 2026, emissions trading systems cover roughly one quarter of global CO₂ emissions, while direct carbon taxes still cover only about 5% to 6%. Slickorps Ventures is more concerned about another issue that can easily be obscured by the figure of 30%: “being priced” and “being priced high enough to change behavior” are two entirely different things. If a tonne of carbon is priced at only a few dollars, it has statistically entered the carbon market, but in corporate decision-making it may still remain close to free. Global carbon policy is addressing the “breadth” of coverage, but what truly determines emissions-reduction investment is the “intensity” of the price.

## Carbon Prices Become a True Market Signal Only When They Enter Corporate Cost Functions  

The economic logic of carbon pricing is essentially to rewrite external costs, which were originally dispersed across society, back into the bills of companies and consumers. The problem is that if this price is too low, it cannot change capital allocation. The materials you provided indicate that actual carbon prices in many markets remain below USD 10 per tonne, while common estimates of the social cost of carbon exceed USD 100 per tonne. The gap between the two is not merely a tenfold numerical difference, but the dividing line that determines whether policy can penetrate corporate decision-making. If the carbon price is only USD 5 per tonne, its impact on the end price of gasoline is even less than 1%, and can easily be overwhelmed by normal fluctuations in oil prices; when the price approaches USD 100 per tonne, its impact on gasoline costs may reach around 15%, at which point companies are more likely to seriously compare the full life-cycle costs of fuel-based equipment and low-carbon alternatives. Slickorps Ventures therefore frames effective carbon pricing in a simpler way: coverage determines how many emissions are seen, while price determines whether those emissions are actually changed.

## 30% Coverage May Create an Illusion of “Policy Completion”  

This is also the most important risk to watch in the present-day carbon markets. As coverage continues to rise, it can easily create the impression among policymakers and capital markets that 30% of global emissions have already entered market mechanisms and that the green transition is naturally accelerating. But if the vast majority of covered emissions remain in low-price ranges, the actual effect may be closer to “establishing a unit of account” than to “establishing an effective constraint.” From an investment perspective, this point is extremely important. Companies will not automatically build expensive low-carbon capacity merely because a carbon market exists. They will compare carbon costs, technology costs, financing costs, and the credibility of future policy; if future carbon prices remain below the cost of transformation for a long period, continuing to emit may still be the more economically rational choice. For Slickorps Ventures, it is therefore not enough to look only at whether a country has an ETS or a carbon tax. More attention should be paid to actual transaction prices, the proportion of free allowances, sectoral coverage, and whether companies can pass carbon costs on to end consumers. The existence of an institution is only the first step. Real marketization occurs only after carbon costs become high enough, stable enough, and credible enough.

## Judgment from Slickorps Ventures: The Core of Future Carbon Market Competition Will Shift from “Covering More” to “Establishing Credible Prices”  

The truly difficult part of the next stage of global carbon pricing may not be raising coverage from 30% to 40% or 50%, but giving emissions that have already entered the system a more genuine price signal. Prices that are too low will not drive technological substitution, while prices that are high but lack political stability may trigger industrial relocation, consumer backlash, and policy reversal. Therefore, a truly mature carbon market must address three issues at the same time: price levels, policy predictability, and international cost differences. Slickorps Ventures sees this as the key stage in which carbon markets move from “policy engineering” to “capital pricing”: only when companies begin to factor future carbon costs into power plant lifetimes, factory site selection, automotive technology pathways, and long-term capital budgets will carbon prices truly become an operating variable similar to interest rates, energy prices, and exchange rates. Around 30% of global emissions already have a price, but the real question is: how many of those prices are actually high enough to change the next investment decision? This may determine the speed of the global low-carbon transition more than coverage itself.
