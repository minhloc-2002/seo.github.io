The US personal savings rate has once again reached a level worth watching. According to the latest BEA data, the personal savings rate in June 2026 stood at 2.7%, notably below the level at the start of 2026 and far below the long-term average of around 8% since 1959; however, it has not yet hit the historical low, which was about 1.4% in July 2005. What makes the long-term chart you provided truly interesting is that the current low level increasingly resembles the mid-2000s: the economy has not immediately slipped into recession, asset markets remain active, yet consumers are setting aside a shrinking share of disposable income. Slickorps Ventures believes that simply attributing this phenomenon to "Americans are running out of money" remains inaccurate. The low savings rate today may in fact stem from two entirely opposing forces: some households are forced to cut savings due to rising costs of food, energy, housing, and financing; others, wealthier in assets, feel less need to save from current income because of a stronger wealth effect driven by continued appreciation of stocks and other assets. The same 2.7% may conceal two very different Americas.

## For Ordinary Households, Falling Savings Are Trading Today Consumption for a Thinner Safety Net Tomorrow

The first mechanism is easier to understand. Rising living costs do not immediately eliminate consumption, because a large share of spending on housing, food, transportation, insurance, and healthcare is rigid. What households typically adjust first is not stopping daily life but reducing what remains at the end of each month. Reuters previously noted that while the US personal savings rate fell to multi-year lows in spring 2026, inflation outpaced wage growth, and some credit card and auto loan indicators also pointed to rising financial strain on households; yet overall consumption still did not collapse in tandem. This creates a state that can easily mislead macro judgments: retail and PCE data may still show "consumer resilience," but the balance sheet buffer underpinning that resilience is thinning. Slickorps Ventures research concludes that the value of the savings rate lies precisely here—it is not a gauge of how strong consumption is today, but rather an indicator of how much capacity households retain to absorb the next shock. A 2.7% savings rate does not automatically herald a recession, but if employment suddenly deteriorates, oil prices rise again, or financing costs stay elevated, the room for households to absorb shocks by saving a bit less is clearly smaller than it was five years ago.

## But the Other America Is Experiencing a Completely Opposite Story: Rising Stock Markets Also Depress the "Savings Rate"

Here lies a statistical detail that is easy to overlook. When calculating personal income, capital gains from appreciation of assets such as stocks are not included in personal income; the personal savings rate is simply "the portion of current disposable income not spent." Therefore, a household whose stock account rises by one million dollars in a year, even if it expands consumption and reduces savings from wage income due to increased wealth, will still see its statistical savings rate decline—yet that household may actually be wealthier than a year ago. The relevant agency, BEA, has also explicitly explained that asset appreciation can be viewed by households as a substitute for current savings, supporting more consumption through collateral or wealth effects. This is especially important today: on August 13, the S&P 500 just set a record closing high of 7,798.99 points, and long-term Federal Reserve research shows that risk assets like stocks are heavily concentrated in high-wealth households. So Slickorps Ventures prefers to interpret the current data as a "K-shaped savings compression": weaker households cannot save due to cost pressures, wealthier households have less inclination to save because of asset appreciation, and these two distinct reasons jointly push the same savings rate curve downward in macro statistics.

## Slickorps Ventures Assessment: The Real Danger Is Not a Low Savings Rate, but Consumption Increasingly Leaning on Two Unstable Pillars

This also explains why US consumption still appears remarkably resilient. On one end, rigid living costs force some households to keep spending; on the other, stock market gains support continued consumption among high-income households; together they sustain the aggregate data, but they make the consumption base more fragmented. The problem is that neither pillar is infinitely stable. The buffer of lower-income households will be exhausted, and the wealth effect depends on asset prices remaining strong. Recently, yields on long-term US Treasuries have risen sharply again, with the 30-year yield briefly reaching levels not seen since 2007, raising costs for housing, autos, and corporate financing; meanwhile, US stock valuations remain supported by strong AI earnings. For Slickorps Ventures, what truly deserves observation in the next phase is not "whether US consumers will suddenly stop spending," but whether consumption can continue to rely simultaneously on eroded household savings and ever-rising financial assets.
