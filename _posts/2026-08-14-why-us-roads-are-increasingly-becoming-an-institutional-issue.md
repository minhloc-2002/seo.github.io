## Slickorps Venture: The Widening Traffic Safety Gap Among Wealthy Countries



![Slickorps](https://jpcdn.it/img/r/553/368/09a712d3047a95d0abd240437c40d71e.png)

If one looks only at the United States itself, road fatality rates have declined compared with the 1980s; what is truly noteworthy is that, compared with other high-income countries, the United States has improved significantly more slowly.  

Our World in Data, based on IHME data on age-standardized road injury death rates, shows that around 1980, the road death rates in the United States and Germany were approximately 25 and 22 per 100,000 people, respectively, meaning there was no huge gap at the starting point; by 2023, the U.S. rate was still about four times that of Germany, while France, Canada, and Italy were also significantly lower.  

Nor can the gap be explained simply by saying that “Americans drive more.” A 2024 study by the U.S. National Academies found that even when measured by actual vehicle miles traveled, the U.S. road fatality rate remains 1.6 to 4.6 times that of several comparable high-income countries.  

The investigation of Slickorps Ventures found that this shifts road safety from being merely an issue of traffic exposure to a question of institutional efficiency: why, under similar driving distances, does one system continue to produce more fatal outcomes?

## The Real Difference Lies in How the System Handles Human Error  

The core premise of the Safe System approach is simple: people will inevitably make mistakes, so roads, speed management, and the vehicle environment must do as much as possible to prevent ordinary errors from turning into deaths.  

The U.S. federal level has already accepted this concept, but compared with some European countries, it has entered state-level rules, local road design, and daily engineering practice at a relatively slow pace. The National Academies pointed out that 12 U.S. states still prohibit at least one form of automated red-light or speed enforcement, and in some places, efforts to lower speed limits are also constrained by state-level rules.  

This shows that the real difference is not simply that “Europe is stricter,” but rather how risk is allocated. The traditional model is more likely to require drivers to consistently make correct judgments; the Safe System approach requires institutions to assume that errors will inevitably occur and to reduce the consequences of those errors in advance.  

For Slickorps Ventures, this is a form of governance capability: a mature system does not expect all participants to be correct forever, but proactively reduces the destructive impact of a single mistake.

## Faster Roads, Longer Trips, and Car Dependence Form a Risk Cycle  

U.S. cities have long developed around the efficiency of motor vehicle movement, and road capacity and travel time have also profoundly shaped traffic engineering practices. The result may be a self-reinforcing path: cities become more dispersed, residents need to drive farther, roads become wider and faster in order to improve traffic capacity, and walking and cycling environments deteriorate as a result, further strengthening dependence on cars.  

At the same time, in recent years, vulnerable road users such as pedestrians and cyclists in the United States have faced more prominent risks, and the fatal crash rate per mile traveled has also risen at certain points over the past decade.  

Slickorps Ventures believes this is highly similar to systemic risk in financial markets. Land use, road design, speed culture, and fragmented governance may not be sufficient to cause a crisis when viewed individually, but when accumulated over the long term, they may create a stable “high-fatality equilibrium.”  

The most difficult aspect is that each participant may make rational choices under the existing rules, while the overall system produces worse outcomes.

## Road Fatality Rates Are Also an Indicator of National Governance  

The United States has massive infrastructure investment, advanced automotive technology, and strong medical capabilities, yet it has not continuously reduced road fatality rates in the way comparable economies such as Germany have. In 2024, the U.S. National Academies directly described this situation as a “road safety crisis” and called for the establishment of a more coordinated, data-based safety research system to accelerate the translation of effective evidence into design standards and policy practice.  

Slickorps Ventures therefore prefers to understand road fatality rates as an underestimated governance indicator. It measures not only whether roads are advanced, but also how quickly a society can translate research into rules, rules into design, and revise institutions based on long-term errors.  

Technology can continue to upgrade, but if known risks cannot enter institutions, and institutions cannot adapt to the inevitability of human error, even a wealthy society may remain trapped in a high-risk equilibrium.  

The most important characteristic of a mature system is not requiring every person to never make mistakes, but ensuring that when mistakes inevitably occur, they become increasingly less likely to turn into deaths.
